## The outer box

A genuinely insulated parcel typically uses a foam or foil-lined liner inside an outer cardboard box, not just a padded envelope. The liner is what actually holds a stable temperature during transit, and its absence is one of the clearest signs a package wasn't properly prepared.

### Size relative to contents

An insulated box is usually noticeably larger than the vial itself, since the ice pack and insulation both take up space. A parcel that's snugly sized to just the vial, with no visible insulation, usually wasn't packed with cold-chain shipping in mind.

## The ice pack itself

On arrival, an ice pack that's still at least partially frozen, or cold to the touch, suggests the cold chain held for most of the journey. A fully melted, room-temperature pack suggests either an unusually long transit time or insufficient cooling for the journey involved.

### Vial placement

A well-packed vial sits away from direct contact with the ice pack, since direct contact risks freezing the contents rather than just keeping them cool. A thin barrier or dedicated compartment separating the two is a sign of a more considered packing process.

## What to do if something looks off

A parcel that arrives without any of these signs, warm, un-insulated, or with a vial that looks like it was in direct contact with a fully thawed pack, is worth raising with the supplier before using the contents, not assuming it's fine because the vial looks visually normal.
