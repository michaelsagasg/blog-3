Order early in the week, before the supplier's stated dispatch cutoff, so the parcel dispatches same day, moves through a single working day of transit, and arrives on a day you know you'll be in. Getting any one of those three out of sync, ordering too late, too close to the weekend, or on a day you'll be out, is what turns a well-packed parcel into one that sits unrefrigerated longer than planned.

## Start with the dispatch cutoff, not the delivery estimate

### Why the cutoff is the real controlling factor

A checkout page might show an estimated delivery date, but that estimate is only accurate if your order goes out same day. Most suppliers state a same-day dispatch cutoff, commonly somewhere between midday and 2pm, and an order placed even slightly after it usually rolls over to the next working day's dispatch run instead. Checking this cutoff before you order, rather than trusting the estimated date shown at checkout, gives you a more realistic picture of when the parcel will actually leave.

### What "same-day dispatch" doesn't include

Same-day dispatch means the parcel leaves the supplier that day, not that it arrives that day. It still needs to travel through whatever courier service the supplier uses, typically one to two working days for standard UK tracked delivery. Confusing the dispatch date with the delivery date is a common reason people expect a parcel sooner than it's realistically going to show up.

## Picking the right day of the week to order

### Early week gives you the most flexibility

Ordering on a Monday or Tuesday means that even if the parcel takes the full stated transit window, or hits a minor delay, there's still room for it to arrive before the weekend. This matters because most UK courier services slow down or pause entirely over Saturday and Sunday, so any delay picked up late in the week tends to compound rather than resolve itself.

### The risk of ordering on a Thursday or Friday

An order placed Thursday afternoon or Friday can easily end up dispatching Friday and not moving again until Monday, turning a normal one-to-two-day transit window into four or five calendar days. If that parcel then arrives on a Monday or Tuesday with nobody expecting a delivery that late, it's more likely to sit unattended for a stretch once it does arrive.

## Matching delivery day to your actual schedule

### Work backwards from a day you're confirmed to be in

Rather than ordering whenever the impulse strikes, check your own calendar first. Pick a day you know you'll be home for most of the day, then count back the supplier's stated transit time, plus a buffer day if you can spare it, to find your ideal order date. This is a more reliable approach than ordering and hoping the delivery estimate happens to land conveniently.

### Building in a buffer for delays

Courier delivery estimates are typically best-case figures. Building in one extra day of buffer, particularly for retatrutide orders where the parcel may include a larger ice pack for a longer expected shelf-stable window, gives you room to absorb a minor courier delay without the parcel arriving on a day you weren't prepared for.

## Reordering before you run out, not after

### Timing a repeat order around your existing supply

If you're managing an ongoing supply, placing a reorder while you still have some remaining stock, rather than after you've run out, gives you the flexibility to time the new delivery around your schedule rather than needing it urgently on a specific day. An order placed under time pressure is more likely to be rushed into whatever delivery slot happens to be available, regardless of whether you're actually going to be in for it.

## Putting the pieces together

Check the dispatch cutoff, order early in the working week, and pick a delivery window that lines up with a day you're confirmed to be home. None of these steps require anything from the supplier beyond what a clear listing should already state, and together they meaningfully reduce the chance of a parcel arriving on a day it ends up sitting outside for hours before anyone brings it in.
