## Most guidance points to 2-4 weeks refrigerated

Once retatrutide powder is mixed with bacteriostatic water, most guidance for similar peptides points to a usable window of roughly 2-4 weeks when the vial is kept refrigerated at 2-8°C throughout. This is considerably shorter than the months-long shelf life of the unopened lyophilised powder, because dissolving the peptide in water gives the degradation reactions, hydrolysis, aggregation and oxidation, a medium to proceed in that they didn't have before.

### Why there isn't one exact number

Peptide manufacturers and compounding sources don't all publish an identical figure, and the number that applies to a specific vial depends on its own documentation rather than a general rule. Concentration, the exact formulation, and the type of water used to reconstitute all shift where in that broad range a given vial actually falls. Where a specific product's own instructions or accompanying paperwork give a figure, that figure takes priority over any general estimate.

## What determines whether a vial lasts closer to two weeks or four

Four factors do most of the work in determining how well a reconstituted retatrutide vial holds up within its stated window.

Consistency of refrigeration matters more than any single dramatic exposure. A vial kept at a stable 2-8°C the entire time tends to hold up better than one that's technically refrigerated but experiences frequent swings from being taken in and out.

Light exposure adds oxidative stress on top of whatever thermal degradation is occurring, so a vial stored in an opaque container or the back of a fridge, away from direct light when the door opens, fares better than one left somewhere it catches daylight regularly.

Handling frequency matters because every time a vial leaves the fridge, however briefly, it warms slightly and then cools again, and those small cycles add up over the course of several weeks of regular use.

The water used to reconstitute matters too. Bacteriostatic water contains a small amount of benzyl alcohol that has a mild preservative effect against bacterial growth, which is part of why it's generally preferred over plain sterile water for a vial that will be used across multiple weeks rather than a single session.

### A realistic weekly pattern

A vial reconstituted at the start of the month and used several times a week, taken out briefly each time and returned promptly to the fridge, is a common and reasonable pattern that fits comfortably within a 2-4 week window. A vial that sits out on a counter between uses, even for what feels like a short time, is accumulating degradation in a way that a vial returned to the fridge immediately after each use isn't.

## Signs a vial may be past its useful window

Visible cloudiness, a colour change, or particles in a solution that started clear are the main visual indicators something has gone wrong, though the absence of any visible change doesn't guarantee potency hasn't declined, since much of the relevant chemistry doesn't produce a visible symptom. A vial noticeably approaching or past the end of its stated window is a reasonable point to be more cautious generally, rather than assuming it's fine because nothing looks different.

## Tracking the window properly

The single most useful habit for managing this is writing the reconstitution date directly on the vial with a permanent marker the moment bacteriostatic water goes in. It removes any guesswork later in the month about exactly how long a given vial has actually been in use, which matters more than it sounds like it should once several vials or a busy few weeks are involved. Some people also keep a simple written log alongside their fridge storage, noting the reconstitution date and the batch or COA reference where one is available, which makes it easier to track multiple vials accurately over time without relying on memory.

## Why reconstituting smaller amounts more often can help

Mixing an entire supply of retatrutide powder into a single large reconstituted batch might seem more convenient than reconstituting smaller amounts as needed, but it puts the whole supply on the same short clock at once. Reconstituting only what's realistically going to be used within a 2-4 week window, and leaving the remaining powder in its more stable lyophilised form until it's needed, keeps a larger share of the total supply under the longer, more forgiving shelf life that unopened powder has. This is a simple planning choice rather than a strict rule, but it's worth considering for anyone holding more than one vial at a time.

### What happens right at the edge of the window

There's no sharp cutoff where a vial goes from fully effective to inert overnight, since degradation is a gradual, cumulative process rather than a switch. A vial at the three-week mark of a four-week window isn't necessarily unusable, but it's reasonable to expect it's carrying more accumulated degradation than it had at week one, particularly if storage conditions weren't perfectly consistent throughout. Being closer to the end of a stated window is a reason for more caution, not an automatic reason to discard a vial outright, and a vial's own documentation or accompanying guidance should still take priority over a general estimate like this one.

## How delivery conditions feed into this window

The 2-4 week estimate assumes the vial started from a reasonably well-handled delivery in the first place. A parcel that arrived after sitting in transit longer than expected, or with signs of a compromised cold chain such as a fully melted ice pack, may already be starting its reconstituted life with more accumulated stress than a promptly delivered one, even before any water is added. This is part of why checking a supplier's packaging and dispatch practices matters beyond just the moment of delivery; it affects how much of that 2-4 week window a vial realistically has left once it's actually mixed and in use.
