Refrigerate peptides at 2-8°C. Don't freeze them, even though freezing feels like the more cautious option to a lot of people ordering for the first time. The instinct makes sense: colder seems safer. For peptides, it isn't, and understanding why comes down to what actually happens to a peptide's structure at freezing temperatures versus refrigerated ones.

## The short answer, and why it isn't obvious

A peptide's function depends on its specific folded, three-dimensional structure, not just its chemical formula. Refrigeration at 2-8°C slows down the chemical reactions that gradually degrade that structure over time, which is exactly what you want from storage. Freezing does something different, and worse: it changes the physical state of the water in the solution around the peptide, which introduces a new kind of damage that refrigeration doesn't.

## What freezing does to peptide structure

When a solution freezes, water molecules arrange themselves into ice crystals rather than staying as a uniform liquid. Those crystals form and grow in the spaces around dissolved molecules, including peptides, and the physical pressure and concentration changes this creates can disrupt the folded structure holding a peptide together. Once thawed, the ice is gone but the structural disruption may not reverse, meaning a peptide can come out of a freeze-thaw cycle with reduced or altered function even though the solution looks completely normal.

### You can't see the difference

This is the part that catches people out: a frozen-then-thawed vial typically looks identical to one that was refrigerated the whole time. There's no visible cloudiness, no colour change, nothing that flags a problem to the naked eye. That's precisely why the advice is to avoid freezing altogether rather than relying on inspection afterwards to catch a mistake.

## Lyophilised powder versus reconstituted liquid

This risk applies specifically to solutions, not to lyophilised, freeze-dried powder. Before reconstitution, a peptide exists in a dry, water-free state, and without water present there's no liquid medium for ice crystals to form in around the molecule. This is why unopened lyophilised vials generally tolerate a wider range of temperatures during shipping, including brief cold exposure, far better than a reconstituted solution does.

### Once water is added, the rules change

The moment bacteriostatic water is mixed into the powder, the peptide is back in a liquid state where freeze-thaw damage becomes a real risk again. From that point forward, the same 2-8°C refrigeration and freezing avoidance applies as it would to any other reconstituted peptide, regardless of how forgiving the powder was beforehand.

## If a vial was accidentally frozen

If a reconstituted vial has been through even one freeze-thaw cycle, whether from a fridge running too cold, an unattended cool box, or a UK winter delivery left outside overnight, the safest approach is to treat it as compromised rather than to inspect it and assume it's fine. There's no reliable at-home way to confirm structural integrity after freezing, and the visual cues used to spot other kinds of degradation, cloudiness or particles, don't reliably show up here.

### Preventing it in the first place

Check where the coldest part of your fridge actually is, since some fridges run colder near the back or bottom regardless of the temperature dial, and a vial pushed too far back can sit close to 0°C without anyone noticing. A basic fridge thermometer left in place for a day or two is a cheap way to confirm the shelf you're using sits reliably within 2-8°C rather than dipping lower.

## Getting the day-to-day storage right

Keep reconstituted vials on a middle shelf rather than against the back panel or near a freezer compartment in a combined fridge-freezer unit. Avoid the door shelf too, though for a different reason: door temperatures swing too high, not too low, with every time the fridge is opened. The goal in both directions is the same, a stable position inside the 2-8°C range, away from both the warmth of the door and the cold of the back panel or ice box.

## The practical rule

If you're ever deciding between a slightly colder setting and a slightly warmer one for storing peptides, refrigerated and stable beats colder and inconsistent every time. Freezing isn't a more careful version of refrigeration, it's a different kind of risk, and treating it that way is the simplest way to avoid an invisible mistake with a reconstituted vial.
