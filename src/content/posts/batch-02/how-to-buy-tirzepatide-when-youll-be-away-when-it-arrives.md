If you know you won't be home when a tirzepatide order is due, either reschedule the delivery to a day you will be, or set up a courier hold-and-collect before it's dispatched. A cold-shipped parcel doesn't wait for you the way a normal parcel does. Once its ice pack starts warming, it keeps warming regardless of when you eventually pick it up.

## The problem isn't the order, it's the gap after delivery

A tirzepatide vial travels inside insulation built to bridge the transit time between the supplier's dispatch point and your address, usually calculated for a one to two day journey. That packaging isn't designed with a further unplanned wait built in. A parcel that clears transit fine but then sits on a porch for the rest of a working day has effectively added a leg to its journey that nobody accounted for when the box was packed.

### What actually happens to the parcel while you're out

On a mild day, a shaded porch adds relatively little extra strain. A parcel left in direct sun, a glass porch, or a black letterbox on a warm afternoon can sit well above the intended 2-8°C storage range within an hour or two. Neither you nor the courier gets any signal that this has happened, since standard courier services don't track or report a parcel's internal temperature at any point.

## Set delivery options before the parcel ships, not after

### Using the courier's app to redirect in advance

DPD, Evri and Royal Mail all offer some form of pre-delivery notification, usually a text or app alert with a narrowed delivery window and an option to change the drop-off instructions. If you already know you'll be out, use this to redirect to a neighbour who's definitely in, a nearby locker, or a specific collection point rather than leaving the default "leave in a safe place" instruction active.

### Choosing "leave with a neighbour" deliberately, not by default

A neighbour who's told in advance that a parcel needs to go in a fridge is a far better outcome than an unannounced parcel left with whoever happens to answer the door. If you're going to rely on a neighbour, have the conversation before the order is placed, not as an afterthought when the courier is already at the door.

## Building your order date around your diary

Look at your own calendar first, then place the order to land on a day you're confirmed to be home, rather than ordering on impulse and hoping the delivery date works out. If a supplier states a same-day dispatch cutoff, for example an order placed before 1pm going out that afternoon, you can usually calculate a fairly precise expected delivery day and pick your order date to match a day you know you'll be in.

### What to do about a multi-day trip

If you're away for more than a day or two, it's usually simpler to delay the order until you're back rather than trying to manage a redirect from a distance. A parcel redirected to a locker still needs collecting within a reasonable window, and a locker sitting unclaimed for several days is no better for a cold-chain item than a doorstep.

## Considering a work address instead

### When a workplace delivery makes more sense

If your household is empty for most of a working day but your workplace accepts personal parcels, switching the delivery address to work rather than home can be the more reliable option, provided someone reliably signs for deliveries there. This works particularly well for weekday orders where you know your work schedule with more certainty than your home availability, and it avoids the parcel being handed to whoever happens to answer a residential door.

### What to check before using a work address

Confirm your workplace's policy on personal deliveries and whether parcels are refrigerated or just held at reception, since a parcel sitting on a reception desk for several hours has the same exposure problem as one left on a doorstep. If there's a staff fridge available and a colleague willing to move it there promptly, a work address can actually outperform a home delivery on a day nobody's in.

## Checking the parcel once you're back

Before using the contents, check that the ice pack still has some cold or partial ice left and that the vial looks visually normal, with no cloudiness or discolouration. If the parcel was left out for an extended period in warm weather and arrives room temperature with no residual cold in the pack, treat that as a reason to contact the supplier rather than proceeding as normal. None of these checks can guarantee potency on their own, but a warm, fully thawed parcel after a long unattended wait is a clear signal something in the chain broke down.
