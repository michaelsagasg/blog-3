## The fridge, not the freezer, is where a reconstituted vial belongs

A reconstituted peptide vial should go in a standard fridge at 2-8°C, not a freezer. This surprises some people, since freezing seems like it should be even more protective than refrigeration if cold slows degradation. For a peptide already dissolved in bacteriostatic water, freezing introduces a different kind of damage that refrigeration avoids: ice crystal formation.

### Why ice is a problem for a dissolved peptide

When a water-based solution freezes, ice crystals form and grow, and that process physically pushes and squeezes whatever is dissolved in the liquid, including peptide chains. That mechanical stress can unfold parts of the peptide's structure and push chains into contact with each other, encouraging the same kind of aggregation that heat causes, just through a different mechanism. Thawing doesn't undo this. A peptide vial that's been frozen and thawed once has typically sustained some of this disruption even if it looks visually normal afterward.

## What repeated freeze-thaw cycles do

The damage from a single freeze-thaw cycle is often modest, but it compounds with repetition. A vial that's frozen and thawed multiple times, for example, moved in and out of a freezer several times over a period of weeks, accumulates disruption with each cycle. This is a well documented issue across protein and peptide storage generally, not something specific to any one compound, and it's the main reason freezing reconstituted vials isn't standard guidance despite cold generally being protective.

### The exception: unopened lyophilised powder

Freeze-thaw damage applies specifically to peptide already in solution. Unopened lyophilised (freeze-dried) powder contains no significant free water for ice crystals to form in, which is a large part of why it's chemically stable for so much longer than a reconstituted vial in the first place. This doesn't mean freezing lyophilised powder is standard practice either, since manufacturers generally specify refrigeration for it too, but the mechanism that makes freezing risky for liquid doesn't apply the same way to dry powder.

## What a standard fridge actually provides

A domestic fridge set correctly runs at 2-8°C, which is cold enough to substantially slow hydrolysis, aggregation and oxidation without introducing the freeze-thaw problem. This is why 2-8°C shows up consistently across peptide storage guidance rather than a colder figure. It's not a compromise between "ideal" and "practical," it's the range that best matches the chemistry involved for a reconstituted vial specifically.

### Where in the fridge matters too

A fridge door sees more temperature fluctuation than the main body of the fridge every time it's opened, since warm air from the kitchen enters and mixes with the cold air inside. Storing a vial toward the back of a middle shelf, away from the door and away from the back wall where it can occasionally freeze against a cooling element, keeps it in the most stable part of the appliance. A basic fridge thermometer is a cheap way to confirm the actual temperature rather than assuming the dial setting is accurate, since many domestic fridges run several degrees off from their displayed setting.

## What to do if a vial has been frozen by accident

A vial pushed to the back of a fridge shelf can sometimes freeze against a cold spot or a cooling plate without anyone intending it to. If this happens once, briefly, it's not automatically catastrophic, but it's a reasonable point to inspect the solution closely for any cloudiness or particles before continuing to use it, and to be more cautious about trusting its remaining potency. If a vial has been frozen and thawed repeatedly or for an extended period, treating it as compromised is the more conservative and reasonable approach, since there's no reliable way to visually confirm how much structural disruption has occurred.

The practical rule that covers nearly all of this is simple: once bacteriostatic water goes into the vial, the freezer is off the table, and a stable position in a standard fridge running at 2-8°C is the setup that matches how these peptides are actually meant to be stored.

## A note on cold-chain delivery versus home storage

The freeze-thaw problem described here applies to home storage after reconstitution, not to a cold-shipped parcel in transit, which relies on an ice pack cooling the outside of the box rather than freezing the vial directly. A well-packed parcel keeps the vial cool without freezing it, the same target range as a home fridge, so there's no conflict between wanting a cold delivery and avoiding a frozen one. If a vial does arrive genuinely frozen solid, rather than just cold, treat it the same way as an accidental freezer mishap at home: inspect it carefully once thawed and be cautious about relying on its full potency.

## A quick comparison to keep in mind

| | Fridge (2-8°C) | Freezer (below 0°C) |
|---|---|---|
| Reconstituted vial | Standard guidance, supports the full usable window | Not recommended, ice crystals can damage the dissolved peptide |
| Unopened lyophilised powder | Standard guidance | Not standard guidance, manufacturers generally specify refrigeration |
| Main risk | Door fluctuation, infrequent monitoring | Freeze-thaw disruption to peptide structure |

### If in doubt, default to the fridge

When storage instructions for a specific product are unclear or missing, defaulting to standard refrigeration at 2-8°C is the safer assumption across nearly every peptide product on the market, rather than assuming colder is automatically better. Freezing is the exception that requires a specific reason and specific instructions, not the default that a cautious buyer should reach for on their own initiative.

## Why this matters when choosing where to keep the vial long-term

Picking a dedicated spot in the main fridge, rather than a shared shelf where the vial might get pushed toward the back wall or the door each time someone else uses the fridge, reduces the chance of an accidental freeze or a door-related temperature swing over the weeks a reconstituted vial is in use. A small, clearly labelled container or storage box kept in the same spot every time makes this an easy habit to maintain rather than something that depends on remembering where the vial was left last.
