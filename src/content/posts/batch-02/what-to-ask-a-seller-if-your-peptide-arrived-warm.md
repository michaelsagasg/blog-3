Photograph everything before you ask anything. If a peptide parcel arrives warm, the first useful step isn't contacting the seller, it's documenting the parcel exactly as it arrived: the outer box, the insulated liner, the ice pack, any temperature indicator, and the vial itself, before you move or open anything further than necessary. That record is what turns a vague complaint into something a seller can actually act on.

## Document it before you do anything else

Photograph the ice pack specifically, since whether it's still cold, partially melted, or fully liquid tells you roughly how much of the transit window the cold chain actually held for. If there's a temperature indicator included, photograph its state clearly and legibly. Note the delivery time against the expected window, and keep the courier's tracking record, since a delivery that arrived a day late explains a warm parcel in a way that an on-time delivery doesn't.

### Don't reconstitute or use the vial yet

If the peptide is a reconstituted solution rather than lyophilised powder, hold off on using it until you've raised the issue with the seller. Reconstituted peptides are far more sensitive to heat exposure than dry powder, and using a dose from a vial you're not confident in removes your ability to return or query it later.

## What to ask the seller directly

### Ask what their packaging is supposed to achieve

Ask what temperature range and transit time their packaging is designed to maintain. This tells you what "normal" was supposed to look like, which you can then compare against what you actually received and how long the delivery took.

### Ask what caused the delay, if there was one

If the parcel arrived later than expected, ask whether the seller was aware of the delay and what, if anything, they did once they knew. A seller monitoring tracking proactively and reaching out before you noticed a problem is a meaningfully different response to one that only engages after you complain.

### Ask what their remedy is

Ask specifically whether they offer a replacement, a refund, or neither, and what evidence they need from you to process it. A seller with a genuine cold-chain guarantee should be able to state this policy without hesitation, since it should already exist as a written process rather than being decided case by case.

### Ask whether this is a known issue on your route

If you're in a part of the UK with longer typical transit times, such as parts of Scotland, Northern Ireland, or certain island postcodes, ask whether the seller adjusts packaging for that route and whether other deliveries there have had similar problems. A pattern specific to a route points to a packaging or courier issue rather than a one-off failure.

## What a reasonable response looks like

A seller who asks for your photos, explains what likely went wrong, and offers a clear next step, replacement, refund, or reshipped order, within a day or two is behaving the way a genuine cold-chain guarantee should work in practice. Slow, generic responses, or a request that you simply use the product anyway, are signs the guarantee exists on paper more than in how the seller actually operates.

### Keep the conversation in writing

Raise the issue by email or a written messaging channel rather than by phone alone, so there's a record of what was asked and what was offered. If a dispute doesn't resolve quickly, that record is what you'd need to escalate through a payment provider or marketplace, separate from the seller directly.

## When to discard rather than argue

Regardless of how the seller responds, if the vial shows visible cloudiness, particles, or an unexplained colour change from what it looked like when freshly mixed, discard it rather than use it. A refund or replacement conversation is worth having, but it shouldn't be the reason a compromised dose gets used in the meantime.

## Using the experience to judge future orders

A single warm delivery, handled well by the seller with a clear explanation and a fair remedy, doesn't necessarily mean their cold chain is unreliable overall; couriers occasionally fail regardless of how well a parcel is packed. A pattern of repeated warm arrivals, or a seller who's slow or evasive when asked these questions, is the more useful signal for deciding whether to continue ordering from them, rather than judging the whole relationship on one isolated incident.

## If the seller doesn't respond at all

If a seller goes quiet after you've raised the issue with photos and specific questions, follow up once more after a reasonable window, two or three working days is typical, before escalating further. Keep that follow-up in writing too, referencing your original message and its date, so there's a clear timeline if you need to take the matter to a payment provider or marketplace for a chargeback or buyer protection claim.

### What payment providers typically want to see

Most card issuers and payment platforms handling a dispute want to see evidence that the item didn't match what was described, which is exactly what your arrival photos, the seller's stated packaging or guarantee claims, and a record of your attempts to resolve it directly are for. Having all of this gathered from the moment the parcel arrived, rather than reconstructed afterwards, makes an escalation considerably more straightforward if it comes to that.

## Using this to inform future orders

A single warm delivery isn't necessarily a reason to write off a seller entirely, especially if they respond well and the cause looks like an isolated courier delay rather than a packaging failure. A pattern of warm arrivals, or a poor response the first time it happens, is more telling, and worth weighing heavily against ordering from that seller again regardless of how the rest of their listing reads.
