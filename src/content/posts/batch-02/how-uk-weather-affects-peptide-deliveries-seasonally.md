## Summer is the highest-risk season, even in the UK

Summer creates the greatest risk to a peptide parcel in transit, and this holds true even though the UK doesn't experience the extreme heat of some other countries. UK summer air temperatures reaching the mid-to-high twenties Celsius are common in July and August, but the inside of a parked delivery van, a letterbox, or an unshaded porch runs considerably hotter than the surrounding air, sometimes 15-20°C hotter in direct sun. A parcel doesn't experience the day's reported temperature; it experiences whatever microclimate it's actually sitting in, and that's frequently far warmer than the forecast suggests.

### Where the real risk sits within a summer delivery

The riskiest points aren't usually the hours a parcel spends moving through a sorting network, since those facilities are indoors and the transit time is typically short. The higher-risk points are a parcel sitting in a hot vehicle during a multi-stop delivery round, and a parcel left on a doorstep or in a porch after delivery, particularly if nobody is home to bring it inside promptly. A delivery scheduled for the morning, before daily heat builds, and collected quickly once it arrives, meaningfully reduces total warm-time compared with an afternoon delivery left outside for hours.

## Winter brings a different, generally smaller risk

Cold UK winters, with regular frost and occasional sub-zero spells, pose a much smaller risk to a well-packed parcel than summer heat does, since the products involved are meant to be refrigerated rather than kept at room temperature anyway. The main winter risk isn't temperature itself but weather-related delays: snow, ice and storms disrupting road networks and slowing the courier network's transit times, which extends how long a parcel spends in transit generally, insulation and ice packs included. A parcel delayed by two extra days because of disrupted roads has effectively had its insulation working for two extra days, which most packaging isn't designed to sustain indefinitely.

### Freezing versus overheating for lyophilised powder

Lyophilised (freeze-dried) powder tolerates brief cold exposure, including mild freezing, considerably better than a reconstituted liquid vial would, since there's minimal free water present for ice crystals to damage. This means an unopened vial sitting in a cold van or an unheated porch on a frosty morning is a far smaller concern than the same vial sitting in a hot van in August. This asymmetry is worth knowing, since it's the opposite of what many people assume when they picture "extreme weather" as the main risk.

## Spring and autumn: the least predictable stretch

Spring and autumn in the UK don't reliably sit in a moderate middle ground; day-to-day and even hour-to-hour temperature swings can be significant, with a cold, overcast morning giving way to unexpectedly strong afternoon sun. This variability makes it harder to anticipate risk from the date alone compared with the more predictable extremes of deep summer or midwinter. A mild-looking day in April or September can still produce a hot conservatory or a sun-trapped porch by early afternoon.

### Regional variation across the UK

Weather conditions, and the resulting delivery risk, aren't uniform across the UK. Southern England tends to see higher peak summer temperatures than Scotland or Northern Ireland, while northern and coastal regions, along with island postcodes, more often see winter weather disruption affecting transit times. Someone in a warmer southern county has more reason to think about summer heat specifically, while someone in a region with a longer standard delivery window has more reason to think about total transit time regardless of season.

## What this means for timing a delivery

None of this is something a buyer can fully control, since weather on a given delivery day isn't predictable weeks in advance. What is controllable is choosing delivery timing sensibly where options exist, being available to bring a parcel inside promptly rather than letting it sit outside, and having realistic expectations that a summer delivery carries meaningfully more thermal risk than a winter one, even though winter brings its own separate set of delays to account for.

## Planning around known weather events

A heatwave forecast a few days out, or a period of severe winter weather affecting a specific region, is a reasonable prompt to think ahead about timing rather than reacting after the fact. Where a delivery date can be chosen or adjusted, avoiding the peak of a forecast heatwave, or building in extra time around a period of forecast snow and ice, reduces the chance of a parcel sitting somewhere unfavourable for longer than necessary. This isn't about avoiding ordering during certain months altogether, since good insulation is designed to handle a normal range of UK weather, but about being a bit more deliberate around known extremes.

### A rough seasonal summary

Taken together, the four seasons each bring a different profile of risk rather than one being straightforwardly worse than the rest. Summer brings the highest heat-related risk and the shortest safety margin once a parcel is out for delivery. Winter brings a lower thermal risk but a higher chance of weather-related transit delays extending how long a parcel spends between despatch and arrival. Spring and autumn sit in between but carry more day-to-day unpredictability than either extreme. Recognising which risk applies to a given time of year makes it easier to know what actually matters for a specific delivery, rather than treating every parcel the same way regardless of when it's arriving.

## What this means for choosing a supplier

A supplier who adjusts packaging with the season, an extra gel pack or a heavier liner through the warmer months, rather than shipping identically all year round, is accounting for exactly the seasonal pattern described above. It's a reasonable question to ask directly if a listing doesn't already say so, since a fixed packaging standard applied regardless of forecast conditions is a weaker match to UK weather's actual seasonal swing than one that flexes with it.
