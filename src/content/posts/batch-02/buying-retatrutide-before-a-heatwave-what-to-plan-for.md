Order earlier in the week and check the supplier's packaging before a heatwave, because a standard ice pack that comfortably holds temperature in normal UK weather can melt well before a parcel arrives when transit vans and depots are running significantly hotter than usual. Planning around the forecast, rather than ordering exactly as you would any other week, reduces the chance of a compromised delivery.

## Why heat affects a retatrutide delivery

A parcel's real thermal environment during transit is often the inside of a delivery van or a depot, both of which can run considerably hotter than outdoor air temperature on a warm day. An ice pack rated to last through a normal one or two-day transit window in typical UK conditions can be exhausted faster when the surrounding temperature is well above what it was designed around.

### Ice pack duration in high ambient temperatures

Standard ice packs are usually specified for a certain duration under moderate conditions, not for extreme heat. During a genuine heatwave, that duration can shrink meaningfully, particularly for a parcel that spends time sitting in a hot van or an unshaded depot rather than moving continuously. This doesn't mean every summer delivery is at risk, but it's a reasonable factor to plan around during a forecast heatwave specifically.

## Planning the order around the forecast

If a heatwave is forecast for the days around when your parcel would normally be in transit, it's worth checking whether you can shift the order earlier, so delivery happens before the hottest days rather than during them.

### Avoiding Friday dispatch into a hot weekend

A parcel dispatched on a Friday, sitting through a hot weekend with reduced courier movement, faces the worst combination of extended transit time and high ambient heat. Ordering earlier in the week, so the parcel is dispatched and delivered on working days rather than sitting over a warm weekend, avoids that specific combination.

## What to ask a supplier during a heatwave

Some suppliers adjust their packaging during known hot periods, using additional ice packs, a different pack type rated for longer duration, or extra insulation. If a listing doesn't mention this, it's reasonable to ask directly before ordering during a heatwave, rather than assuming standard packaging is automatically adjusted for the conditions.

### Questions worth asking directly

Asking whether the packaging changes seasonally, and whether the supplier can confirm the specific pack type being used for your order that week, gives you a clearer picture than assuming "cold shipped" means the same thing in July as it does in January.

## If you'll be away during the hot spell

Ordering to arrive on a day when you know you'll be home matters more during a heatwave than at any other time of year. If a forecast hot spell overlaps with days you'll be out, or away entirely, it's worth delaying the order until you're back, rather than risking a well-packed parcel sitting on a doorstep in full sun for several hours. A short delay in ordering is a smaller risk than a parcel arriving into an empty house on the hottest day of the week.

### Checking the forecast a few days out, not just on the day

Weather forecasts for four or five days ahead are reasonably reliable for spotting a heatwave coming, which gives enough notice to shift an order earlier or later if needed. Checking the forecast when you're about to place an order, rather than only reacting once the heat has already arrived, is what actually gives you the option to plan around it.

## Being ready to refrigerate the moment it arrives

On your side, being available to bring the parcel in and refrigerate it as soon as it's delivered matters more during a heatwave than at any other time of year. A parcel left on a doorstep in direct sun for even an hour after delivery, on top of a transit that's already used up most of the ice pack's capacity, is a much worse outcome than the same parcel brought indoors within minutes.

If you know you won't be home to receive a delivery during a forecast hot spell, it's worth checking whether the supplier offers a safe-place or neighbour delivery option, or simply timing the order for a day you know you'll be in, rather than letting a well-packed parcel sit outside in the heat after it's already arrived.
