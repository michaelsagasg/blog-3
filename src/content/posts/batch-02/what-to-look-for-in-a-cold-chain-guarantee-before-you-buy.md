A real cold-chain guarantee names a specific temperature range, states exactly what remedy you get if the parcel arrives outside it, and says what evidence you need to provide. If a seller's guarantee is missing any one of those three, what you're reading is a reassuring line rather than an enforceable policy, and it's worth checking before you order rather than finding out after a warm delivery.

## What counts as a real guarantee versus a marketing line

"We guarantee cold-chain delivery" is a marketing line. It sounds like a commitment but doesn't actually commit the seller to anything specific if a delivery goes wrong. A real guarantee reads more like a policy document than an advert: it states conditions, a process, and an outcome, in that order, the same way a returns policy for any other product would.

### A quick test for any guarantee you read

Ask yourself whether you could hold the seller to the exact wording if a parcel arrived warm tomorrow. If the wording is vague enough that the seller could reasonably argue either way, it isn't doing the job a guarantee is supposed to do.

## The temperature range should be specific

A genuine guarantee states the range the product is meant to stay within during transit, commonly something in the region of 2-8°C for a temperature-sensitive peptide, or the phrase "did not exceed" a stated upper limit if the packaging is designed to tolerate brief warmer periods. A guarantee that just says "kept cold" or "temperature controlled" without a number attached gives you nothing to check the delivery against, and nothing for the seller to be held to either.

### Why the number matters more than it seems

Without a specific figure, a warm-feeling parcel and a seller's judgement of "still cold enough" can disagree, and there's no objective standard either side can point to. A stated range turns that disagreement into a simple comparison instead, particularly if the parcel also includes a temperature indicator that shows whether the range was exceeded.

## The remedy should be spelled out

Look for the specific outcome offered if a delivery fails the guarantee: a full refund, a free reshipped replacement, or a choice between the two. Vague language like "we'll make it right" or "get in touch and we'll sort it" leaves the actual outcome entirely up to the seller's discretion on the day, which isn't the same as a stated policy.

### A timeframe matters too

A guarantee worth trusting also states how quickly the seller responds and resolves a claim, whether that's within 24 hours, a few working days, or some other stated window. An open-ended "we'll get back to you" gives the seller no accountability for how long you're left waiting on a vial you may not be able to use.

## Evidence they'll accept from you

A clear guarantee states what you need to provide to make a claim: usually photos of the parcel, the ice pack's condition, and any temperature indicator, taken promptly on arrival. Knowing this in advance matters, because it tells you to document the delivery immediately rather than realising after the fact that you should have photographed the melted ice pack before putting it in the bin.

### Reasonable versus excessive evidence requests

A request for photos taken on arrival is reasonable. A guarantee that requires something impractical, an unboxing video for every order, or evidence you'd have no reason to have gathered without being told in advance, functions as a guarantee that's difficult to actually claim on, even if it reads well on the page.

## Red flags in a guarantee

Watch for guarantees that apply only in cases the seller decides are "reasonable," without defining what that means, or that require you to raise a claim within an unrealistically short window, such as the same day as delivery regardless of when you actually open the parcel. Both effectively shift the guarantee's real-world value closer to zero while still allowing the seller to advertise having one.

## What a strong guarantee looks like in practice

A UK peptide seller with a genuine cold-chain guarantee will typically state, in plain terms on their shipping or FAQ page: the temperature range promised, the remedy offered if it's not met, the evidence needed, and a reasonable window to raise a claim. If you can find all four in writing before you order, you're dealing with a policy. If you're relying on the word "guarantee" alone, you're dealing with a hope.
